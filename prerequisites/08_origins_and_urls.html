Origins & URLs

Two office buildings can sit on the exact same street, one door apart — but "the same street" doesn't mean "the same security badge works at both." What actually determines whether your badge works is a precise triple: which building, which company owns the lease (protocol/trust level), and which specific floor's access system (port) you're presenting the badge to. Change any one of the three, and it's a different building for badge purposes — even if a human glancing at the street would call it "basically the same place."

Every URL decomposes into named parts, and the web platform's entire security model is built on exactly three of them, together called the origin:

Origin = (scheme, host, port). Everything after the port — path, query string, fragment — is irrelevant to origin comparison.

The Parts, Precisely

  • Scheme: https vs. http are different origins — the trust level (encrypted + authenticated vs. not) is part of the identity, not a detail.
  • Host: the exact hostname. example.com and accounts.example.com are different hosts — a subdomain is not automatically "the same site" for origin purposes (it can be, deliberately, for the narrower "site" concept — see below).
  • Port: example.com:443 and example.com:8080 are different origins, even on the identical host, identical scheme.

Why the Browser Defines Identity This Way

The origin tuple isn't an arbitrary convention — it's chosen because each piece maps to a genuinely different real-world trust boundary: scheme maps to "was this connection encrypted/authenticated at all," host maps to "who actually controls the DNS name and the TLS certificate for it," and port maps to "which specific listening service on that host." Two origins differing in any one of the three can be run by completely different, mutually untrusting parties, so the browser treats them as such by default — that default is the Same-Origin Policy (Module 17).

"Same-Origin" vs. "Same-Site" — Not the Same Idea

Two terms that get conflated constantly, so pin them down now:

  • Same-origin: scheme, host, and port all match, exactly. https://a.example.com and https://b.example.com are not same-origin (different hosts).
  • Same-site: a looser, cookie-relevant notion — roughly, the registrable domain matches (using the Public Suffix List to know where "the site" actually ends, e.g. that .co.uk isn't itself a registrable suffix). Under same-site, https://a.example.com and https://b.example.com are considered the same site, because they share the registrable domain example.com.

Same-origin is the stricter check used by the Same-Origin Policy for script/DOM/fetch access. Same-site is the looser check used by cookie SameSite attributes (Module 29). Mixing these two up is one of the most common terminology errors in web security discussions — a full vocabulary-web page on this exact confusion lives alongside Module 29 once it's built.

http://example.com and https://example.com look almost identical to a human skimming a URL bar, but they are different origins by definition. If a demo shows an SOP violation "unexpectedly" succeeding or failing, check the scheme first — it's the part people's eyes skip over.
